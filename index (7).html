The words we read past

Bengali stop words for natural language processing: a categorised list of 317 words, a Python module, and what the list reveals about the language.

Simanta NandiMA, Digital Humanities and Social Sciences, Indian Institute of Technology (Indian School of Mines) Dhanbad

Move the light across the dark to read.Drag your finger across the dark to read. Stop words are the small words every sentence leans on and every reader passes over. Here, in the dark, they are the only words there are.

সারসংক্ষেপ

Abstract

Every Bengali sentence leans on a handful of small words. Pronouns, postpositions, conjunctions, auxiliary verbs and negators carry the grammar of a sentence while saying little about what it is about. In natural language processing (NLP) these are called stop words, and they are usually removed before a text is counted, indexed or modelled.

English has had tested stop lists since the early years of information retrieval (Luhn 1958; Fox 1990). Bengali, with roughly 284 million speakers (Eberhard, Simons and Fennig 2025), has far fewer, and most of those in circulation are bare lists in Bengali script that say nothing about how the words were chosen.

This project compiles 317 Bengali stop words in romanised form and annotates each one with its Bengali-script spelling, an English gloss, a grammatical category and a register label. The words fall into 17 categories, grouped into four families: pronominals, relational words, adverbials, and verbal forms with negation. The list is released as a Python module in the format set for the course, as plain-text, CSV and JSON files, and as this website.

Three findings stand out. Pronouns and their case forms make up 42.6 per cent of the list, because Bengali marks person, number, case and three grades of politeness on the pronoun itself. The romanised list does not map cleanly onto Bengali script: 317 entries resolve to 314 distinct Bengali words, and one entry could not be matched with confidence. And a test on a short passage shows the list removing a little over half of all tokens (27 of 49), while words carrying suffixes pass through untouched. I argue that an Indic stop list is more useful when it records categories and register, so that a researcher can decide which kinds of small word to remove for a given question.

Keywords Bengali; stop words; natural language processing; text preprocessing; Indic languages; romanisation; digital humanities

উদ্দেশ্য

Objectives

  1. Compile a Bengali stop-word list that covers the closed-class words of standard modern usage, together with the older literary forms that still turn up in digitised books.
  2. Pair every romanised entry with its Bengali-script spelling, a short English gloss, a grammatical category and a register label, so that the list can be checked rather than taken on trust.
  3. Package the list as a Python file in the format set for the course, with small functions for filtering text written in either script.
  4. Analyse the make-up of the list and report what it shows about Bengali grammar and about the limits of romanised stop lists.
  5. Publish the data, code and analysis openly under a Creative Commons BY-NC 4.0 licence.
গবেষণা-প্রশ্ন

Research questions

RQ1
Which kinds of words make up most of a Bengali stop list, and what in the grammar of Bengali explains that balance?
RQ2
How far does an informal romanisation map onto Bengali script, and where does the mapping fail?
RQ3
How much of ordinary prose does a fixed list remove, and which forms escape it?
RQ4
Beyond the words themselves, what should an Indic stop list record if it is to serve humanities research as well as engineering?
পদ্ধতি

Method

The work moved through six stages. Each stage fed the next, and the final annotated table is the single source for every file and figure in the project.

  1. Collection

    I began with the personal pronouns and wrote each one out through its case forms, singular and plural. Relative and interrogative pronouns followed, then postpositions, conjunctions, adverbs of time and place, quantifiers, auxiliary verbs and negators. The list grew over several rounds of revision.

  2. Romanisation

    Entries are written in the informal romanisation that many Bengali speakers use when typing on a phone or a Latin keyboard, not in a scholarly scheme such as ISO 15919. I kept the spellings as I first typed them. The variation in the list is part of the evidence, so I did not tidy it away.

  3. Script mapping

    Each romanised form was matched to its standard Bengali spelling. Honorific pronouns keep their chandrabindu (তাঁর, এঁর, ওঁর), and the distinction between কি, the yes-or-no particle, and কী, 'what', is preserved.

  4. Annotation

    Every entry received a category, a gloss, a register label (standard colloquial Cholito, literary Sadhu, or regional) and, where needed, a note. Twelve entries carry a note, usually because the form is ambiguous or its spelling is uncertain.

  5. Checking

    A script counts the entries, looks for duplicates, finds romanised forms that collapse into one Bengali word, and runs the list over a short test passage written for this project.

  6. Release

    The same table generates the Python module, the plain-text, CSV and JSON exports, the figures in the repository and the charts on this page, so the numbers cannot drift apart.

Where the words fall

Pronominals dominate, with 135 of the 317 entries. Adverbials follow with 80, relational words with 59 and verbal forms with 43. The largest single categories are the proximal and distal pronouns and the postpositions, which have 31 entries each. Select a segment to open those words in the list.

Why the pronouns multiply

An English stop list needs four words for the first person singular: I, me, my, mine. Bengali needs more, mostly because it builds politeness into the pronoun. The second person alone comes in three grades. Tui is for close friends, younger siblings and children; tumi for equals and most family; apni for strangers, elders and anyone owed respect. The third person splits into ordinary and honorific forms (se and tini), and the same split runs through the near, far and relative series (Thompson 2012).

Each grade then takes its own nominative, objective and genitive, in the singular and the plural. Thirteen pronoun series across six case slots make 78 cells, and the list fills every one of them. Two gaps sit just outside the grid. The bare near pronoun e (এ) is missing, so its nominative slot is held only by the literary iha. And the everyday classifier form ekta (একটা) is absent although the more formal ekti is present.

Five initial sounds, one system

Sort the list by first letter and three letters lead: k (54 words), t (46) and j (32). This is no accident of spelling. Bengali builds its question words, relative words and answering words as matched sets that differ only in the opening sound. Kothay asks 'where?', jekhane means 'where' in a relative clause, sekhane answers 'there', and ekhane and okhane point to 'here' and 'over there'. Grammars call this the correlative system.

Laid out as a table, the system also shows what the list has missed. Kokhon ('when?') is absent, though kokhono ('ever') is present. So are eto and oto ('this much', 'that much') and omon ('like that'). These are frequent words and good candidates for the next version.

Old and new registers

Twenty-eight entries (8.8 per cent) belong to Sadhu Bhasha, the formal literary standard of nineteenth-century prose, which the colloquial Cholito standard has since largely replaced in writing. Almost all of them are pronouns: tahar beside tar, iha beside e, jaha beside je, and the long objective amadigoke beside amaderke. They matter for anyone working with digitised literature from before the middle of the twentieth century, where these forms are ordinary function words. Only two entries are marked as regional: kamne ('how') and koy ('how many'), both common in eastern speech.

Short words, mostly

The romanised forms average 4.96 letters, with a median of five. The shortest is o, a single letter that is both a pronoun ('he' or 'she', at a distance) and the particle 'also'. The longest run to ten letters: the literary plurals tomadigoke and apnadigoke, and the past perfect forms hoyechhilo and korechhilo.

From roman letters to Bengali script

Romanised Bengali has no fixed spelling, and the list shows it. Two pairs of entries spell one Bengali word: onno and onnya (অন্য), and jothto and jothesto (যথেষ্ট). One entry, noba, matches no standard form I could confirm. The list is also inconsistent with itself. It writes chilo with one h but chhilam and hochhilo with two, so a romanised text that spells the word chhilo slips straight past the filter.

Bengali script avoids that problem, because its spelling is settled, but it sets a different trap. Letters such as য় and ড় can be stored either as one Unicode character or as a base letter followed by a dot below, the nukta. The two look the same on screen and do not match inside a computer. The Python module converts every text to Unicode Normalisation Form C before comparing words, which removes the mismatch (Unicode Consortium 2024).

Look at what survives. শহরটা ('the town') and শহরটাকে carry a classifier and a case ending, so they are no longer bare nouns, which is what a content word should look like after filtering. But একটা is a classifier form that ought to go and is not on the list, and গেল ('went') and মনে (as in মনে হওয়া, 'to seem') behave much like function words without being listed as such. A stemmer, or a list that includes common suffixed forms, would catch more.

আলোচনা

Discussion

A stop list looks like a neutral tool, but it is a small theory of what counts as empty in a language. For English the theory costs little. Function words carry little that a topic model or a search index needs, and even so, web search engines have moved over time from long stop lists to short ones and, in many cases, to none (Manning, Raghavan and Schütze 2008). For Bengali, the assumption runs into trouble in at least three places.

The first is politeness. Removing tui, tumi and apni throws away information about how the people in a text stand towards one another. A study of dialogue in fiction, of forms of address in letters, or of how two characters slip from apni to tumi as they grow close would lose its main evidence. This is why the list is published with categories. A researcher can drop the postpositions and conjunctions and keep the pronouns.

The second is negation. Bengali usually places the negator after the verb (jabo na, 'I will not go'), and deleting na, nei or noy turns a sentence into its opposite. For sentiment analysis, or any task that depends on what a sentence actually asserts, the negation category should stay in the text.

The third is script. A great deal of Bengali written online is romanised, typed on Latin keyboards with no agreed spelling. A list in Bengali script alone misses that material entirely; a romanised list without variants misses much of it. Publishing both forms side by side is a modest step towards handling the two together.

Taken together, these points answer RQ4. For digital humanities work the useful question is seldom whether to remove stop words. It is which ones, for this question, in this corpus. Categories and register labels make that choice visible and easy to change, which a flat list of words cannot do.

সীমাবদ্ধতা

Limitations

The list rests on my own knowledge of the language and on standard grammatical description. It has not been checked against word frequencies from a large corpus, so some common function words are probably missing (the analysis found ekta, kokhon and the bare pronoun e), and a few entries may be rarer than their place here suggests.

The romanisation is informal and far from exhaustive. Real romanised text varies much more than this list does, with ch and chh, o and a, s and sh alternating freely. A filter that relies on exact matches will miss many spellings that any reader would recognise at once.

Matching works on whole words only. Bengali attaches emphatic particles (-i, -o), classifiers (-ta, -ti, -gulo) and case endings directly to the word, so আমিই ('I myself') will not match আমি unless a stemmer runs first.

The standard colloquial Bengali of West Bengal dominates. Bangladeshi standard usage and regional varieties, including the Manbhumi speech of the country around Dhanbad, are barely represented.

A few forms are uncertain. Twelve entries carry a note, and three of them (noba, aposh and jothto) should be checked against a dictionary before the list is reused.

The list knows nothing of context. It cannot tell kal 'yesterday' from kal 'tomorrow', or o 'he' from o 'also', and it removes both senses alike. Finally, the test passage is short and was written for the purpose. Its figure of 55 per cent illustrates the method; it is not a benchmark.

পরবর্তী কাজ

Further work

  • Check the list against word frequencies from a large Bengali corpus such as AI4Bharat's IndicCorp (Kakwani et al. 2020), adding frequent forms that are missing and marking rare ones.
  • Compare it with existing Bengali stop lists, such as the one in the Stopwords ISO collection, and report where they overlap and where they part.
  • Add a light stemmer for emphatic particles, classifiers and case endings, and a transliteration step, so that romanised and Bengali-script text pass through one pipeline.
  • Fill the gaps the correlative table exposed: kokhon, eto, oto, omon, ekta and the bare pronoun e.
  • Ask a small group of Bengali speakers from West Bengal, Bangladesh and the Jharkhand border to review the list and its register labels.
  • Extend the same annotated format to the other Indic languages covered in the course.

Using the code

Download bengali_stopwords.py and place it beside your own script. It needs nothing beyond the Python standard library.

# Filter a Bengali sentence in either script
from bengali_stopwords import bengali_stopwords, remove_stopwords, is_stopword

print(len(bengali_stopwords))   # 317

text = "আমি যখন প্রথমে এখানে এসেছিলাম, তখন শহরটা আমার কাছে একটু অচেনা ছিল"
print(remove_stopwords(text))
# ['এসেছিলাম', 'শহরটা', 'অচেনা']

print(is_stopword("tumi"), is_stopword("তুমি"))   # True True

To keep a category in the text, for instance the pronouns of address in a study of dialogue, build a custom list from the categories:

from bengali_stopwords import bengali_stopwords, stopword_categories

keep = set(stopword_categories["Second-Person Pronouns"])
keep |= set(stopword_categories["Negation"])
custom_list = [w for w in bengali_stopwords if w not in keep]
print(len(custom_list))   # 287
